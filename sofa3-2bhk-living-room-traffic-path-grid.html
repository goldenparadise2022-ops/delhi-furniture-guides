3-Seater Sofa in a 2BHK Living Room: Traffic Path Grid

2BHK living rooms usually connect the entrance, kitchen and bedrooms. Where a three-seater sits decides whether those routes stay clear or turn into an obstacle course. Use it alongside your own measurements and household routines rather than as a fixed rulebook.

Tick-box list

  • Sketch the room with all doors marked.
  • Draw each daily route as a line.
  • Place the sofa where no line crosses it.
  • Check the door swing of every room.

Putting it into practice

Draw the routes

Drawing lines from each door to where people actually go shows which walls are free. The sofa belongs where routes do not cross.

Balcony doors

Many 2BHKs place the balcony off the living room. Keep the sofa clear of the sliding track or door swing.

Keeping it practical

Every household is different, so adapt these suggestions rather than following them rigidly. What suits a couple in a studio may not suit a joint family, and the person who uses the sofa most should have the loudest say.

A family in a Ghaziabad 2BHK might find that the only wall free of routes is opposite the kitchen, which then becomes the sofa wall.

Delhi homes vary widely, from compact DDA flats and builder floors to large NCR apartments. Measure the clear floor rather than trusting brochure areas, and note doors, switchboards, windows and AC units before you shortlist anything. Write your final numbers on a single page and carry it when you visit showrooms or compare listings online.

Mistakes that are easy to make

  • Copying a layout from a photo taken in a much larger room.
  • Blocking a window, balcony door or cupboard that is used daily.
  • Skipping the tape-on-the-floor test because the numbers 'look fine'.

Step-by-step approach

Start with a rough sketch, add the fixed elements such as doors and windows, then pencil in the sofa. Tape the outline on the floor, walk every route with something in your hands and adjust before you shortlist models. Share the notes with everyone at home before ordering, since a second opinion often catches something missed.
